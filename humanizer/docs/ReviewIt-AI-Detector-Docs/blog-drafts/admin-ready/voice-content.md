Most of us know the feeling. You read a paragraph back and it is correct, tidy and completely lifeless. Nothing in it is wrong, yet it could have been written by anyone. If you used an AI tool for part of a draft, this feeling is even more common, because the text it produces tends to be smooth, balanced and careful in a way that real writing rarely is.

Your readers notice this, even if they cannot say why. A teacher marking fifty essays knows a distinctive voice when they meet one, and they know when it is missing. This article explains why flat writing happens, and gives you a method for editing a draft so that it sounds like you.

## Why does generic writing feel generic?

Flat text usually has a few predictable features. Once you can name them, you can fix them.

- **The same sentence shape, over and over.** Many sentences open with a subject, follow with a verb and end at roughly the same length. The rhythm becomes a drone.
- **Safe, general words.** Words like "crucial", "significant", "various" and "landscape" fit everywhere, so they say nothing in particular.
- **Stock connectors.** "Furthermore", "moreover" and "in conclusion" turn up in huge quantities, often where a simple "and" or "but" would sound more natural.
- **No specifics.** The text refers to "studies" and "experts" without naming a study or an expert.
- **No opinion.** Every point is balanced and nothing is argued. Real writers take a position.

None of these is a crime. They are just signs that nobody has been at the text yet, adding the things only a person can add.

## Start with what is yours

Before you change a word, ask what you actually know and think about the topic. Spend two minutes writing down, in plain language and without worrying about style, the three things you most want the reader to take away.

This is the most useful step in the whole process. When you have your own points in front of you, you can compare them with the draft. Where the draft says what you think, keep it and polish it. Where it says something you would not say, change it. Where it is silent on something you care about, add it.

## Edit in layers

Trying to fix everything in one pass leads to a muddle. It works better to take the draft through a few short passes, each with one goal.

### Pass 1: meaning

Read for sense only. Does each paragraph make the point you want? Delete anything you cannot stand behind, and anything you could not explain if asked. If a claim has no source and you are not sure it is true, find a source or cut it.

### Pass 2: specifics

Go through and replace general statements with exact ones. "Research shows that exercise improves mood" becomes the actual finding, the people it was tested on and the citation. "A number of countries have introduced rules" becomes the names of the countries. Specifics are the single biggest difference between writing that feels human and writing that does not.

### Pass 3: rhythm

Look at the shape of your sentences. If three in a row are about the same length, change one. Join two short sentences with a comma or a dash. Split a long one in half. Put a very short sentence after a long one when you want to land a point. Reading aloud helps a lot, because your ear hears monotony that your eye misses.

### Pass 4: voice

Now read for personality. Replace stock connectors with whatever you would naturally say. Where it suits the assignment, let a first-person view appear: "I would argue", "What surprised me was". Remove words you would never use in conversation, unless they are the correct technical terms.

### Pass 5: protect the facts

Finish by checking that nothing important changed while you were editing. Numbers, names, dates, quotations and citations must be exactly right. It is surprisingly easy to alter a figure while smoothing a sentence.

## Choosing a style on purpose

Not all writing should sound the same. A lab report, a cover letter and a short story each have a different voice, and mixing them up is a common reason a text feels off.

Humoro's Humanizer offers six writing styles: academic, business, creative, casual, narrative and technical. Before you rewrite a sentence, decide which of these fits the document:

- **Academic** for essays, theses and journal-style writing. Formal, precise, and cautious in its claims.
- **Business** for reports and professional emails. Clear, direct and brief.
- **Creative** for expressive writing where imagery and variety matter.
- **Casual** for blogs, newsletters and informal posts.
- **Narrative** for stories and personal accounts.
- **Technical** for documentation and explanations of how something works.

Choosing a style first saves you from the common trap of making every document sound like a vaguely formal blog post.

## Rewrite only what needs it

One of the worst editing habits is rewriting everything. If a paragraph already sounds like you, leave it alone. Changing it for the sake of changing it often makes it worse, and it throws away work that was already good.

## A worked example

Here is an invented sentence of the kind that tends to feel flat:

> "It is important to note that social media has had a significant impact on various aspects of modern society."

Notice how many places it could appear. Now apply the passes:

- **Meaning:** what do you actually want to say? Perhaps that students check their phones during lectures.
- **Specifics:** which impact, and on whom? "Students who keep social media open during lectures retain less of what they hear" is something you can support with a source.
- **Rhythm:** the original is one long, even sentence. A shorter one will be stronger.
- **Voice:** you might say, "In my own seminar group, almost everyone had a feed open."

The result is shorter, more concrete and unmistakably written by someone. Nobody needs a tool to see the difference.

## Keeping one voice across a long document

Voice is easiest to hold in a paragraph and hardest to hold across thirty pages. Over a long piece, drift creeps in. The opening chapter is lively because you were fresh, the middle gets mechanical, and the ending rushes.

These habits help:

- **Write a one-paragraph style note for yourself.** Something like: "Direct, first person where allowed, short sentences when making a claim, longer ones when explaining." Check each chapter against it.
- **Re-read the opening of the document before editing the end.** It reminds your ear what you were aiming for.
- **Edit in a different order.** Sometimes starting with the last section and working backwards stops you from running out of energy on the part readers remember most.
- **Take breaks.** Voice problems are much easier to hear after a night away from the text than straight after writing it.
- **Be consistent about conventions.** Spelling variety, number formats, how you refer to sources and whether you use "we" or "I" all contribute to the feeling of one voice.

## Using other people's feedback

A second reader is worth more than any amount of polishing. Ask a friend, a classmate or a tutor to mark the places where the writing stops sounding like you. People are surprisingly good at spotting this, and they often point to precisely the sentences you secretly doubted.

When you receive feedback:

1. **Look for patterns, not single comments.** If three different readers stumble in the same paragraph, the paragraph needs work.
2. **Ask "what would you have said?"** Their spoken answer is often clearer than their written comment.
3. **Keep your own judgement.** You are allowed to disagree with a suggestion. Just be sure you can say why.
4. **Thank them, and give credit where your institution expects it.**

## A before-and-after paragraph

Here is a short invented paragraph before editing:

> "Climate change is a significant challenge facing society today. It is important to take various actions to address this issue. Many experts agree that governments, businesses and individuals all have a role to play. In conclusion, collective action is essential to ensure a sustainable future."

Every sentence is acceptable and none is memorable. Now consider a version written by someone who has done the reading and has a view:

> "Flood defences in my own city were designed for storms that now arrive every few years. That mismatch is the clearest argument I know for acting early. Councils can adjust building rules, firms can report their emissions honestly, and households can cut waste, but only the first two have the power to change what gets built. The sooner planners treat the old storm figures as out of date, the less this will cost."

What changed?

- It opens with a concrete fact, not a general label.
- It takes a position, with a reason.
- The sentences vary in length and structure.
- It distinguishes between who can do what, instead of listing everyone.
- It ends on a practical point, not a slogan.

You could not have produced this version without knowing something about the subject, and that is exactly why it sounds like a person wrote it. No tool can supply that knowledge for you. What a tool can do is help you find the sentences that need this kind of attention.

## Questions people ask

**Will rewriting change my meaning?**
It can, which is why you should always read each rewrite. Humoro protects citations, numbers and names so that those stay exact, but you are the final judge of whether the sentence still says what you meant.

**Do I have to use a style?**
Choose the one that fits your document. If you are not sure, academic is the usual choice for essays and theses.

**Should I rewrite every flagged sentence?**
Not necessarily. Start with the ones that matter most to your argument, and leave anything you are comfortable defending.

**What if I do not like the rewrite?**
Do not use it. Edit it further by hand, or keep your original sentence. The tool serves you, and not the other way round.

**Is this a way to avoid doing the work?**
No. Editing is part of the work. The thinking, the reading and the argument have to come from you. Rewriting only helps the finished text express them better.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Swapping synonyms.** Replacing "important" with "crucial" changes nothing.
- **Adding errors on purpose.** Typos do not make writing sound human. They make it look careless.
- **Making it longer.** Padding is a common reason for flat text. Shorter is often better.
- **Losing the argument.** Make sure that your main point survives every edit.
- **Skipping the rules.** Your institution may have a policy on AI-assisted work. Follow it, and say when you are required to.

## When you are done

Read the whole piece aloud one last time. Does it sound like something you would say, on a good day, to someone you wanted to convince? If so, it is ready.

You can also run the AI Detector on the final version, as a quick way to spot any sentences that still read as machine-written. No detector is perfect, so treat the result as a hint about where to look, not a verdict on your work.

Good editing is not about hiding where a draft came from. It is about making sure the finished piece contains your thinking, your evidence and your voice. If it does, it is yours.
