You finish an essay, read it through once, and press submit. Then a small worry creeps in. Some of your sentences came from a tool you used for ideas, and others you rewrote so many times that you can no longer tell which is which. Would your own teacher read it and think a machine wrote part of it?

Checking your own work before you hand it in is a sensible habit, in the same way that spell-checking is. This guide shows you how to do it calmly, what the results mean, and what to do next. It is written for students and academics who want their work to be honestly their own.

## Why check at all?

Many universities and schools now have rules about AI use. Some allow it for brainstorming and ban it for the final text. Others ask you to say when you used it. A few ban it completely. Whatever your institution's rule is, the safest position is to know exactly what is in your submission.

A check is also useful for a second reason: it shows you where your writing has become flat. Text that reads as machine-written usually has a familiar feel. The sentences are smooth, evenly shaped and full of safe, general phrases. Even when you wrote them yourself, reading them back through a detector can show you where your voice went missing.

## Step 1: Start with your finished draft

Run the check on the version you plan to submit, not on an early outline. Changes you make later can introduce new problems, so a final check is worth more than an early one.

Paste your text into the AI Detector, or upload a PDF, DOCX or TXT file. If your document has a reference list, you do not need to remove it. Humoro is built for academic writing, so headings, citations and reference entries are not what it is looking at.

## Step 2: Read the highlights, not just a verdict

A single "AI or human" answer for a whole essay is not very helpful. If someone told you that your 2,000-word paper was "partly AI", you would have no idea where to look.

That is why Humoro checks your text sentence by sentence and colours the sentences that read as AI-written. Instead of one vague answer, you get a map. You can see at a glance whether the flagged sentences are scattered, grouped in one paragraph, or mostly in the introduction and the conclusion.

Patterns in that map are often more useful than the highlights themselves:

- **A cluster in the introduction or conclusion.** These are the parts people most often write on autopilot. Openers like "In today's fast-moving world" and closers like "In conclusion, it is clear that" are easy to produce without thinking.
- **A cluster in one section.** If one section is flagged and the rest is not, that section may have been written in a different way from the others. Perhaps you pasted something in, or perhaps you were tired when you wrote it.
- **Scattered single sentences.** These are usually general statements. They are true enough, but they could appear in any essay on the topic.

## Step 3: Decide what each flag means

A flagged sentence is not an accusation. It is a prompt to look again. Ask yourself three questions about each one:

1. **Did I write this?** If you wrote it yourself, there is nothing dishonest here. The sentence just happens to read in a way that detectors associate with machine writing. You may want to rephrase it anyway.
2. **Did a tool write this, and am I allowed to use it?** If your institution permits it, make sure you have followed the disclosure rules. If it does not, rewrite the sentence in your own words, from your own understanding.
3. **Does this sentence say something specific?** Sentences that could belong to any essay are the weakest, whoever wrote them. A flag is a chance to replace a general statement with a specific one.

No detector is perfect. Treat the results as a signal that helps you decide where to spend your editing time, not as proof of anything. If you disagree with a flag on a sentence you wrote, trust your own knowledge of how you wrote it.

## Step 4: Fix flagged sentences properly

There is a bad way and a good way to deal with a flag.

The bad way is to swap a few words for synonyms. This tends to make the sentence worse and often does not change how it reads. The good way is to change the sentence by putting something of yourself into it.

Here are some approaches that work:

- **Add a specific detail.** Replace "Many studies show that sleep is important" with the actual finding you relied on, with its citation.
- **Change the shape.** If every sentence in a paragraph is about the same length, join two of them, or split a long one. Variety in rhythm is one of the clearest signs of a human writer.
- **Use your own examples.** A real case from your course, your lab or your own experience is difficult to mistake for generic text.
- **Say it the way you would say it.** Read the sentence out loud. If you would never speak it, rewrite it until you would.
- **Cut filler.** Phrases such as "it is important to note that" usually add nothing. Deleting them often fixes the sentence.

If you would rather get help with the rewrite, the Humanizer works only on the sentences you choose. You pick one of six writing styles, and your citations, numbers and names are protected, so the meaning stays yours. Whatever you do, read the result and check that it still says what you meant.

## Step 5: Check again

After you edit, run the check a second time. This is not about chasing a perfect result. It is a quick way to confirm that your changes did what you intended and that you did not introduce new problems elsewhere.

A good rule is to stop once the flagged sentences are ones you can honestly defend. You do not need to remove every highlight. You need to be confident that the essay is your own work and says what you want it to say.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Checking only the first page.** Run the whole document. Problems often hide in the middle.
- **Editing only to change the score.** The aim is better writing, not a different colour on the screen.
- **Treating a clean result as permission.** A check does not replace your institution's rules. Follow them.
- **Pasting in someone else's work.** Only check your own writing. Other people's work is theirs.
- **Leaving it until the last minute.** A check is useful when you still have time to rewrite. Try to leave at least a day before the deadline.

## What flagged sentences tend to look like

You will recognise flagged sentences more quickly once you know the habits behind them. These are invented examples, but they show the common types.

- **The sweeping opener.** "Throughout history, humanity has always sought to understand the world around it." It sounds grand, and it says nothing about your topic.
- **The stacked qualifier.** "It is important to note that, in many cases, various factors may potentially contribute to the outcome." Four hedges in one sentence leave no claim at all.
- **The tidy triple.** "The programme is efficient, effective and sustainable." Lists of three adjectives, each close in meaning to the others, appear everywhere in generic text.
- **The summary that repeats.** "In conclusion, this essay has shown that…" followed by a restatement of the introduction, word for word.
- **The empty bridge.** "This leads us to the next point." Transitions that point at the structure instead of adding meaning.

If you find yourself nodding at these, you do not need a tool to tell you what to fix. Replace each with a sentence that carries information: a claim, a number, a name, an example or a reason.

## Checking long documents

A thesis or dissertation is a different job from a five-paragraph essay. A few habits make it manageable.

- **Check chapter by chapter.** A short, focused run is easier to act on than a single enormous one, and you can fix one chapter before moving to the next.
- **Pay extra attention to chapter openings and closings.** These transition passages are often written quickly, and they tend to collect the stock phrases described above.
- **Keep a short log.** Note which sections you edited and why. If anyone later asks how a passage was produced, you will have a clear answer.
- **Recheck after big revisions.** Merging chapters, accepting a supervisor's edits or pasting in new material can change the feel of a section.
- **Leave the reference list alone.** Humoro is built for academic writing, so entries in a bibliography are not what it is looking for.

## Questions students often ask

**Is checking my own essay allowed?**
Checking your own writing is generally fine. What matters is what you submit and whether it follows your institution's rules. If you are unsure, ask your instructor or read the academic integrity policy.

**What if a sentence I wrote myself is flagged?**
It happens. Detectors work on patterns, and plain, formal writing can resemble machine writing. Use the flag as a prompt to ask whether the sentence could be more specific or more distinctive, and keep it if you are happy with it.

**What if nothing is flagged?**
That is a good sign, though not a guarantee about how any other tool or reader will judge your work. Keep your notes and drafts, because they are the best evidence of how you worked.

**Should I aim for zero highlights?**
No. Aim for an essay you can defend, sentence by sentence. A few highlights on sentences you have thought about and chosen to keep are not a problem.

**Can I check a paper that has several authors?**
Yes, and it is a good idea. Different people write in different ways, so a joint paper can have patches that feel unlike the rest. A final check helps you smooth them into one voice.

It is reasonable to ask what happens to your paper. Humoro does not use your text to train models. Uploaded files are deleted once they have been processed.

## A simple routine you can reuse

If you want something to remember, use this routine for every important piece of writing:

1. Finish your draft.
2. Check the whole document.
3. Look at where the flags are, not just how many there are.
4. For each flag, decide whether you wrote it, whether you may use it, and whether it says something specific.
5. Rewrite with your own details, examples and rhythm.
6. Check once more, then submit with a clear conscience.

Writing with AI tools is now part of life for many students, and the rules around it are still being worked out. Whatever your institution decides, the habit of checking your own work and putting your own thinking into it will serve you well.
