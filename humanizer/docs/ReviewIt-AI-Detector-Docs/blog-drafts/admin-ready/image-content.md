A few years ago, a photograph was usually a record of something that happened. Today, a convincing picture of almost anything can be produced in seconds from a short description. The results are good enough that careful people are fooled every day.

If you use images in coursework, research, a presentation or a social post, it helps to have a calm, practical way of checking them. This guide covers the clues you can look for with your own eyes, the extra checks that take a minute, and how an image checker fits in. It also explains why a result is a signal and not a verdict.

## Why it matters

Images carry authority. A photo in a report suggests that something really looked that way. If the picture was generated, that suggestion is false, even if nobody meant to mislead.

This matters in several settings:

- **Coursework.** Some assignments ask for original photographs, or ask you to say how every image was made.
- **Research.** A generated image presented as an observation is a serious problem, whatever the intention.
- **Journalism and sharing.** Passing on a generated picture as real spreads false information.
- **Your own work.** If you use generated images, you should know which ones they are, and label them honestly.

## Clues you can see yourself

Generation tools are improving, so no clue is certain. But a careful look often turns up something. Zoom in and check these areas.

### Hands, teeth and small details

Fine detail is hard to get right. Look for extra or missing fingers, fingers that merge, teeth that blur into each other, or jewellery that changes halfway across a wrist. Newer tools are better at this, but the mistakes have not disappeared.

### Text and signs

Lettering in an image is a classic weak point. Look at shop signs, book spines, labels and posters. Generated text is often nearly right but not quite: letters that look like letters but do not form words, or words with an odd extra stroke.

### Backgrounds

The subject of a picture tends to be sharp and well formed, while the background can fall apart. Check for lines that bend where they should be straight, railings that stop for no reason, patterns that repeat too neatly, and objects that melt into one another.

### Lighting and shadows

Real light behaves consistently. Ask whether every shadow falls the same way, whether reflections match the objects they reflect, and whether the light on a face matches the light on the surroundings.

### Texture

Skin that looks airbrushed, fabric without a believable weave, or surfaces that are oddly smooth and glossy can all be hints. Real photographs usually have small imperfections, such as dust, noise and uneven wear.

### The overall feel

Sometimes a picture is simply too perfect. Everything is tidy, balanced and well lit, with no clutter. A real photo of a real place usually has something untidy somewhere in the frame.

## Checks that take a minute

Looking closely is only part of the job. These steps give you more to go on.

1. **Find the source.** Where did the image come from? A picture with no origin, no photographer and no date deserves more caution than one with a clear record.
2. **Search for it.** A reverse image search can show where else the picture appears, and whether it was published earlier somewhere that makes the claim about it impossible.
3. **Look at the file details.** Some files carry information about the camera or software used. This can be missing for many reasons, so absence proves nothing, but a camera model or an editing history can help.
4. **Check the context.** Does the picture fit the event it claims to show? Weather, clothing, signs and buildings should match the place and time.
5. **Ask the person who shared it.** If someone gave you the image, ask how it was made. An honest answer is often the quickest route.

## How to read a result sensibly

Whatever tool you use, keep these points in mind.

- **It is a signal, not proof.** No detector is perfect. A confident result is useful, and it still should not be the only thing you rely on.
- **Edited photos can confuse it.** A real photograph that has been heavily retouched, filtered or compressed may look different from an untouched one. Generated pictures can also be edited so that they look more natural.
- **Small or low-quality images are harder.** The less detail a picture has, the less there is to examine.
- **Combine evidence.** A detector result, a source check and a close look at the details together are far stronger than any one of them.
- **Be fair to people.** If a check suggests that someone's picture is generated, do not accuse them on that basis alone. Ask them first.

## Special cases worth knowing about

Some kinds of image are harder to judge than others. Keep these in mind.

- **Screenshots and diagrams.** These are not photographs, so many of the usual clues do not apply. Charts and diagrams can also be generated, and generated charts often contain labels or numbers that do not match anything real. Always check the data against its source.
- **Heavily edited photos.** Retouching, filters and compression change what a picture looks like. A real photograph that has been edited many times can resemble a generated one.
- **Small, cropped or low-quality images.** Less detail means fewer clues. If you can, ask for a larger or original version.
- **Pictures of people.** Faces, hands and hair attract the most scrutiny, and the most mistakes. They also raise questions of consent and privacy, so be careful about what you share and why.
- **Artwork and illustrations.** Generated art can be convincing, and human artists sometimes work in styles that look generated. Do not assume. Ask about the artist and the process.
- **Old photographs.** Scanned prints and restored photos have their own marks of age. Restoration tools may add detail that was never in the original.

## How to label and credit images

Honest labelling prevents most problems before they begin.

- **Photographs you took:** say so in the caption if the context calls for it, and keep the original file.
- **Photographs from others:** credit the photographer and the source, and make sure you are allowed to use the picture.
- **Generated images:** state clearly that the image is generated, and name the tool if your institution or publisher asks.
- **Edited images:** say what was changed if the changes affect what the picture shows.
- **Data visualisations:** cite the data, not only the software that drew the chart.

Many courses and journals now have their own rules about this. Read them early, so that you do not have to redo your figures at the last minute.

Trust the evidence you can verify. If you took the photograph yourself, you know where it came from, and your original files and notes are your strongest proof.

## If you use generated images yourself

There is nothing wrong with using generated images where your institution or client allows it. The honest approach is simple:

- Label them clearly as generated.
- Say which tool you used, if the rules ask for it.
- Never present a generated image as a photograph of a real event.
- Do not use generated images of real people without thinking carefully about consent.

## A quick checklist

Keep this list handy:

1. Where did it come from?
2. Do hands, text and backgrounds hold up when I zoom in?
3. Is the lighting consistent?
4. Does a search show an earlier or different source?
5. What does the image checker say, and is the result conclusive?
6. Do all the signals agree? If not, treat the image as unverified.

## The bigger picture

Spotting generated images is becoming a skill, much like checking a source for a written claim. The tools will keep changing, and so will the clues. What will not change is the habit of asking where something came from and whether the evidence supports it.

Use your own eyes first, add a second opinion from an image checker, and be honest about what you do and do not know. That approach will serve you well, whatever the technology looks like next year.
